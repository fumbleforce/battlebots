# Making weapons look, sound and feel good

Lessons from building the Atlas MX turret family (#36, 23 September 2026): the
cannon, plasma gun, their twin/quad upgrades, flamethrower, Tesla arc and
railgun. The user reviewed each weapon in game and sent feedback in several
rounds. This records what the user asked for, what failed first, and what
finally worked. It complements the construction and material guide in
[STYLIZED_INDUSTRIAL_ASSETS.md](STYLIZED_INDUSTRIAL_ASSETS.md). Implementation
and contracts: [B_ATLAS_TURRET.md](../coordination/B_ATLAS_TURRET.md).

The short version: **a weapon is a model, a rule, a feel and a sound, and all
four must agree.** A well-modelled gun that goes "tap" or a loud gun that
does not move the tank both read as weak.

## 1. Modelling a weapon part

**Size it against the machine, not in isolation.** The first turret looked fine
in a close-up but was "WAY too small" on the hull: a cupola, not a main
turret. Tank turrets in the user's references span most of the hull width and
are boxy, tall and armored. Judge scale in a wide hero view of the whole
machine and at gameplay camera distance. Close-ups hide proportion errors.

**Follow the references' construction language.** The accepted turret is a boxy
casting with vertical walls and a chamfered roof edge. Two armored cheeks
frame a recessed embrasure, a block mantlet sits between the cheeks, and the
casting carries side grilles, a hatch, a sensor and lifting eyes. A tapered
round cupola did not match the brief, however well it was detailed.

**Attachments must look integrated, not bolted on.** Side-pod quad guns hung off
the turret flanks looked "really odd". The fix was armored sponsons that are
part of the turret casting: the same enamel, chamfers, bolts and roof
fairing, with a front embrasure the barrels elevate through. Compact gun
cradles centred on the trunnion stay inside the sponson at every elevation.
Ask of every add-on: *what is it mounted to, and would it survive a hit?*

**Hollow things must be hollow.** "The cannon lacks a hole": a capped cylinder
plus a dark disc does not read as a bore. Build the barrel as a thick-walled
tube (the lathe profile returns along the inside) and the muzzle brake as an
open frame with a pierced front face and real side ports.

**Avoid geometry that pokes out or clips.** A bright crown ring wider than the
brake clipped the barrel and read as a "white disc". Brake-bolt heads stood
proud and looked like nuts. Use low countersunk hardware on barrels, keep
rings inside the silhouette they sit on, and build parts from one coherent
outline.

**Surfaces need the same baked wear as the hull.** A smooth lathed barrel baked
clean, because the hull's chipping follows sharp topology edges and a round
tube has almost none. It looked "wrong compared to the baked surfaces". The
bake now accepts per-material wear properties (`atlas_streak`, `atlas_scratch`,
`atlas_grime`, `atlas_face_wear`): lengthwise drawn-tube streaks, sparse heat
oxidation and denser sleeve chipping. Tone them down if they start to look
like granite. Painted thermal sleeves, clamp bands and a bolted evacuator give a
barrel the hull's enamel-and-hardware language.

**Audit clearance, then turn the result into rules.** The generator sweeps every
moving group at 5° yaw and many elevations against the imported hull GLB. It
measures the lowest clear elevation per bearing and verifies the runtime rule
at 2.5° steps. The measured profile became gameplay:
`AtlasGeometry.TURRET_DEPRESSION`, regenerated by
`tools/update-turret-geometry.py`. The authoritative ray uses the exported
muzzle markers, and a test checks that the GLB muzzle positions match the
server geometry. Consequences to watch for:
- A flat −5° limit (from one corner socket) meant nothing closer than about
  28 m could be hit. A per-bearing profile allows −20° over the nose.
- Wider or lower attachments trade depression: the quads reach only about −2°
  on the flanks. Record trade-offs like this instead of hiding them.
- Optional deck modules the barrel sweeps through (exhausts, top armor) are
  hidden while a turret is fitted. They are cosmetic, so gameplay never
  depends on them.

## 2. Effects: make it read, then keep it readable

**Start bold, then tone down.** Nearly every first pass went wrong one way or the
other. The plasma "read as extremely weak... a little light tap". The first
fix then blew up into screen-filling white blobs. Checklist:
- Additive glow multiplies with bloom. Size flashes in metres of game world
  (the Atlas runtime scale is ×3) and keep additive energy moderate. Give the
  bright part a small hot core, not a large sphere.
- Particle sprites need an explicit small mesh size. Default quad sizes and
  fast velocities scattered glowing "balls" across the arena.
- Don't fill a bright scene with lit smoke. Plasma vapour lit by the bolt's own
  light became white clouds; the plasma now leaves no smoke.
- Dark colors vanish under additive blending. A fire jet that fades to dark
  red disappears halfway. Keep it orange until it is nearly gone, and let
  billows start small and grow along the jet.

**Layer every shot: muzzle, flight, impact, aftermath.**
- Muzzle: a flash cone plus a small hot ball, a short light and a volumetric
  smoke burst. Use lit, depth-softened billow particles (the #31 plume shader),
  not flat grey quads ("just some grey squares").
- Flight: a visible projectile with its own travelling light and trail (shell
  smoke, plasma embers, railgun beam), so the shot is readable from the side.
- Impact: a light burst, sparks/molten spray and a projected decal that stays
  (a plasma burn cools white→orange→char; a cannon leaves a big scorch).
  Cannon adds a fireball, a ground shockwave ring and a rising smoke column.
- These effects are cosmetic and driven by accepted snapshots. Confirmed hits
  still come from combat events.

**Smooth what the player's eye tracks.** The turret and reticle were "choppy":
they followed a 60 Hz authoritative angle at a higher render rate. Estimate
the rate from each new server sample, predict between samples and ease toward
the prediction. Filter the reticle's screen point lightly so ray hits on
uneven geometry do not jitter.

**The camera is part of the weapon.** A crosshair centred on your own hull is
unusable ("absolutely atrocious"). Turret builds view through a tank sight
above the turret. The crosshair lands in the world, the turret chases it, and
a second ring shows where the barrel actually points. Tank buttons follow the
genre: LMB fires the main gun.

**Damage numbers come from the confirmed event, never a prediction (#85).**
`scripts/presentation/damage_numbers.gd` throws one number per accepted hit
with damage above zero, turret shots included. The number shown is the
damage actually dealt after armour. Every hit gets its own number: summing
rapid hits read worse than a spray. Numbers go up and sideways only. The
first version also pushed them along the hit normal, but that normal points
at the attacker, so the numbers flew into the player's camera. Rams report
the attacker's centre, so their numbers start where a ray from that centre
meets the victim's hull, the actual collision point. Text keeps its
on-screen size at any camera distance, and hits on your own bot use the
incoming colour. Tuning is in `data/damage_numbers.json`. Settings → GAME
has separate toggles for numbers on other bots and on your own bot.

## 3. Sound: low, punchy, layered

The user listened to rendered WAVs (`paplay`) and judged each iteration. The
lessons:
- **Weight lives below 150 Hz.** "Too high pitched, not punchy enough." The
  plasma arc went from 1100→130 Hz down to 520→70 Hz, and the bright hiss was
  cut almost entirely.
- **Punch comes from a saturated low transient.** A sine thump with a fast pitch
  drop (cannon 60→28 Hz, plasma 95→38 Hz) through `tanh` saturation,
  plus a very short noise crack at the start.
- **Layer by function:** crack (attack), boom/thump (body), low-passed noise push
  (air), a long rumble (space), then one mechanical detail (breech clank,
  capacitor whine, electrical buzz). Normalise the peak at the end.
- **Give the impact its own sound** at the hit position: a detonation, a
  sizzling burn or a zap. The report at the gun alone feels one-sided.
- **Continuous weapons need loops and state:** a seamless flame-roar loop faded
  by trigger state, and a railgun whine whose pitch follows the charge. Pool
  enough voices so a four-shell volley does not cut itself off.
- Render and play the actual output before calling it done. You cannot judge
  a synthesised sound from its code.

## 4. Recoil and usage: the machine must react

**Recoil moves the whole tank, on the server.** "A real cannon really jolts the
whole tank." Each shot applies a linear impulse at the muzzle opposite the
shot, plus a torque impulse across the shot direction, so the hull rocks back.
Visual camera shake alone is not enough, and a small linear push alone gave
**0°** of visible rock against the stiff drive suspension. Measure it in a test
scene (peak tilt, settle time, stays grounded) rather than guessing.

**Scale it, cap it and let volleys stack.**
- Single cannon about 6–8°, twin 12–15°, quad 18–23°, settling within about 1 s.
- Stacked impulses compound: uncapped, a quad volley flipped the tank (94°).
  No further rock is added past a tilt limit, so heavy weapons feel violent but
  cannot defeat the player.
- The sight camera adds an immediate punch-in and shake for the shooter, which
  hides network latency on the physical jolt.

**Each weapon gets a distinct usage verb.** Cannon: press, volley, long reload.
Plasma: hold, stream, heat-limited. Flamethrower: hold, close cone. Tesla:
point near, auto-arc and chain. Railgun: hold to charge, release to fire,
early release cancels, pierces. A distinct verb makes a weapon memorable and
creates tactics.

**Multi-barrel guns must converge.** Parallel outboard barrels straddled the
target, so half the shots missed while the crosshair sat on it. Barrels now aim
at the point the centre bore line strikes.

## 5. Grabbing, lobbing and front tools (#52–#56)

The harpoon, mortar, ram, spear/forklift and grinder added lessons of their own.
See [B_ATLAS_WEAPONS.md](../coordination/B_ATLAS_WEAPONS.md).
- **Pulls need a speed limit, not just a force.** A constant harpoon pull fired
  the target into the shooter and shoved both 20 m. A winch that drives the
  line toward a reel speed, eases off over the last metres and stops short
  reads as powerful and stays controllable.
- **Holds need damping and a way to break.** The impaled target rides a capped
  spring-damper to the tines. Strain, time, overheat and letting go all
  release it, and letting go throws it.
- **Say what the replicated state is, not what the weapon does.** One generic
  grip (target and world anchor) and one tool pose serve the tether, the
  impalement, the carriage lift and the arm raise.
- **A lobbing weapon changes the camera.** The mortar gets a high artillery
  view with a live arc and blast ring rather than a crosshair on the hull. Its
  command stays a bearing plus elevation, because fixed muzzle speed makes
  elevation the range control.
- **Scale blasts, not sparks.** The bigger mortar detonation first scaled its
  spark sprites too, and they read as glowing balls. Keep sparks gun-sized.
- **Tools share one mount and one audit.** Every front tool bolts to one
  coupler plate, placed clear of the optional chin armour (the first draft
  clipped it). The generator checks every travel extreme against the hull
  and each optional module.
- **The practice dummy sits about 13 m away.** That is inside the mortar's
  minimum range and the harpoon's stop distance, so evidence runs must allow
  for it rather than tune gameplay to the dummy.

## 6. When adding a new part — checklist

1. Brief: role, usage verb and range; which references it follows; how it
   mounts to the machine.
2. Model at the machine's scale and review a wide hero view and a gameplay-
   distance view before fine detail. Build real openings and supports.
3. Bake with the hull's material language, adding per-material wear where
   topology gives none. Check close-ups for clean, smooth or odd pieces.
4. Run the clearance audit. Turn the result into gameplay limits and record
   any trade-offs.
5. Put tuning in `battlebots/data/*.json` through a typed loader (here
   `data/turret_weapons.json` / `TurretTuning`), never as bare numbers in logic.
6. Layer the effects (muzzle, flight, impact, aftermath), sized in world metres,
   then capture natively in the real arena and tone down whatever blows out.
7. Synthesise or record layered sound, render it and listen.
8. Add server recoil/rock and measure tilt and settle time with a cap. Add
   camera feedback for the shooter.
9. Tests: authoritative behaviour (hits, occlusion, allies, rates, heat),
   model/ray contract (muzzle markers match), input path, and native captures.
   Run every test that counts presets or parts: `scorpion_garage_test` failed
   in CI after the presets changed.
10. Catalogue/gameplay changes bump `WireCodec.BUILD` and migrate saved builds;
    pushing to `main` deploys the server.
11. Show it to the user and iterate. Most of the good results here came from
    the second or third round of feedback.
